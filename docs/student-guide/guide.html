1What EPIVUE is

EPIVUE is a small kiosk that photographs one spot on someone's skin and says, in plain words, whether it would be wise to show that spot to a doctor.

It runs on a Raspberry Pi with a camera and a touch screen. It works with no internet, so it can be used at a barangay health event. It does not diagnose. It is a screening aid: its job is to help people decide to get a spot checked, especially when they would not have gone otherwise.

HOW TO SAY IT
"EPIVUE is an offline screening aid. It looks at one skin spot, checks the photo is usable, and tells you in plain words whether to get it checked by a doctor. It never says a spot is safe."

The screen has two sides:

  • The visitor side — plain words, no percentages, no medical jargon, never a green "safe".
  • The staff side ("Details for staff →") — the numbers: model scores, the decision line, A B C values, the heatmap, the photo-check measurements.

3The journey of one photo

Everything happens in a few steps. Each step has a job, and some steps can stop the photo early.

Take photo→Photo check
~70 ms→Heatmap
~1 s, shown on screen→"Check this spot"→Model, 4 views
~0.6 s→A B C
~10 ms→Words on screen
  1. Take the photo. The visitor puts the spot inside the ring on screen.
  2. Photo check. Is there skin? Is it sharp and lit? Is there one spot, inside the ring? If not, the scanner says why (e.g. "TOO BLURRY TO READ") and asks for a new photo. The model is never asked about a bad photo.
  3. Heatmap ("Where it looked"). If the photo passed, a small coloured circle appears showing where the model's attention went, and whether that is on the spot.
  4. The model reads the spot. It looks at the photo four times (as-is, mirrored, upside down, turned around) and averages the answers.
  5. A B C are measured on the outline the photo check drew.
  6. The words. One of a few fixed messages, like "NOTHING STOOD OUT" or "SEE A DOCTOR SOON".

WHY "BY SPOT" MATTERS
Many spots were photographed several times. If one photo of a mole is in training and another photo of the same mole is in the test, the test is too easy — the model has already seen that mole. An early version of this project made that mistake and 40 % of test spots had leaked. Now every photo of one spot stays on the same side (we split by lesion_id).

The external benchmark (the fair exam)

On top of the test split, there is a frozen set of 602 photos from places the model was never trained on (including the 2016 ISIC challenge set and the 99 photos the team collected in October). It is used to compare models fairly and is never trained on. A new model is only allowed onto the Pi after it is measured on this set.

5The model: EfficientNetB0

The "brain" of EPIVUE is a convolutional neural network (CNN) called EfficientNetB0, made by Google researchers in 2019.

What a CNN does, simply

A CNN looks at a picture in layers. The first layers find tiny things — edges, dots, colour changes. Middle layers combine those into textures and shapes. The last layers combine those into "patterns that look like a melanoma" or "patterns that look like an ordinary mole". At the end it gives three numbers that add up to 100 %: how much the photo looks benign, pre-cancerous or malignant.

EPIVUE's model, layer by layer

StepSizeWhat happens
Input224 × 224 pixels, 3 coloursThe photo is shrunk to a small square. Raw colour values 0–255 go in (the model scales them itself).
EfficientNetB0 body~4 million weightsThe layers that find edges → textures → shapes. Starts from a version pre-trained on ImageNet (1.2 million everyday photos), then re-trained on skin.
Last feature map7 × 7 × 1280A 7-by-7 grid over the photo; each square has 1,280 "pattern detectors". The heatmap is read from here.
Average pooling1280 numbersAverages the grid into one list.
Dropout 0.3 → Dense 128 → Dropout 0.3128A small extra layer. Dropout switches off random parts during training so the model does not just memorise.
Output (softmax)3 numbersbenign · pre-cancerous · malignant, adding to 100 %.

On the Pi the model is a TensorFlow Lite file, models/skin_classifier.tflite, only 5.1 MB. TensorFlow Lite is a small version of TensorFlow made for phones and small computers.

How it was trained

  • Transfer learning. Phase 1 (20 rounds): the pre-trained body is frozen and only the new top layers learn. Phase 2: the whole network is fine-tuned, stopping early when it stops improving.
  • Balanced sampling. Pre-cancerous photos are rare (3,191), so they are shown more often so the model does not ignore them.
  • Focal loss. A way of scoring mistakes that pays more attention to hard examples.
  • Camera-style augmentation. Photos are randomly rotated, flipped, blurred a little, made brighter or darker — so the model copes with real camera photos, not only perfect hospital ones.
  • About 1 hour 40 minutes on the RTX 4060.
WHY EFFICIENTNETB0?
It is small and fast enough to run on the Pi's CPU in under a second, while still being accurate. The training notebook can also build the bigger EfficientNetB2, but it is slower on the Pi, so B0 is what ships.

6How the scanner decides

The model gives three numbers. The decision uses only one rule.

THE RULE
cancer score = p(pre-cancerous) + p(malignant)
If the cancer score is 0.47 or more → "flagged": the screen tells the person to get it checked.

Why 0.47? It was chosen so that, on the validation photos, the scanner flags 90 % of the real cancers. A lower limit catches more cancers but sends more harmless spots to the doctor; a higher limit does the opposite. For a screening tool, missing a cancer is worse than an extra visit, so the limit leans toward catching cancers.

The messages a visitor can see

MessageWhen

The staff drawer shows three bars. They are "temperature-scaled" to look honest, but the device never decides by them. In October the team scored 100 photos by the tallest bar and got 43 right; scored by the real rule (cancer score ≥ the limit), the same photos gave 74 of 100 flagged. The drawer now prints the real decision line: DECISION: cancer score 0.62 · limit 0.47 → flagged.

7How good is it? (the metrics)

First, what the words mean

MetricPlain meaningExample
SensitivityOf all the real cancers, how many did it flag?0.81 = it flagged 81 of every 100 cancers.
SpecificityOf all the harmless spots, how many did it correctly leave alone?0.59 = it left 59 of every 100 harmless spots alone; the other 41 were sent to get checked.
AccuracyHow often the single label was right.Not the best measure here — see below.
ROC-AUCHow well it ranks cancers above harmless spots, from 0.5 (coin toss) to 1.0 (perfect).0.89 = good on its own test; 0.75 = fair on the hard external set.

Why not just accuracy? If 9 of 10 spots are harmless, a "scanner" that always says "harmless" is 90 % accurate and useless. For screening, sensitivity (catching cancers) matters most.

"The June model was trained on one hospital's dermoscopy photos and missed most cancers in ordinary camera photos — the kind the Pi takes. The October model was trained on nine collections. 8The photo check (the "gate")

The model has no answer for "this is not a skin spot". Shown a wall, it would still pick benign, pre-cancerous or malignant. So a photo check runs before the model.

CheckQuestionLabel if it fails"Check it anyway"?
1. SkinIs enough of the photo skin-coloured?SKIN NOT RECOGNISEDNo
2. ReadableSharp enough? Light enough, not glaring?CAN'T READ · BLURRY / DARK / GLARENo
3. SpotOne distinct mark, inside the ring, on its own?NOT A SPOT · NONE FOUND / PLAIN SKIN / SEVERAL MARKS · SPOT · NOT IN THE RING / TOO CLOSEYes
4. EdgeDoes the mark have a real edge (a mole stops; a shadow fades)?SPOT · LOOKS LIKE A SHADOW / EDGE HARD TO FOLLOWYes

When the photo passes, the label is SPOT FOUND and the outline the check drew is shown on the photo, so everyone can see which mark is being read.

BE HONEST ABOUT THIS
Several limits in the photo check are marked GUESSED in the code: they were set on test photos, not on real Pi captures. The 30 October session is the first time real Pi photos are collected to measure them. A palm in dim webcam light, for example, can come out as "SKIN NOT RECOGNISED".

9A B C D E — the warning signs

Doctors check moles with the "ABCDE" rule. EPIVUE measures three of the five on the outline the photo check drew. These signs never change the verdict — the model decides; the signs help a person see what to look at.

LetterDoctors look at…How EPIVUE measures itNormal / borderline / stands out
A AsymmetryAre the two halves different?Turns the outline level, folds it along both axes, and measures how much does not line up (0 = mirror image).below 0.15 / 0.15–0.30 / above 0.30
B BorderIs the edge ragged or blurred?Compares the edge length to the area: perimeter² ÷ (4π × area). A perfect circle scores 1.0; a ragged edge scores higher.below 1.8 / 1.8–2.5 / above 2.5
C ColourSeveral colours inside?Groups the colours inside the spot (k-means in the Lab colour space); groups too close together are merged.1 colour / 2 / 3 or more
D DiameterBigger than 6 mm?Not measured. Millimetres need a known scale; this camera has none. Shown as "no scale".—
E EvolvingHas it changed over time?Not measured automatically. Save scans of one spot into a group; "Compare" shows the first and latest photo side by side for a person to judge.Teal "History" arc when a spot has 2+ saves

On screen, the five letters are arcs around the ring, coloured by tier (grey = normal, tan = borderline, red = stands out, blue = not measured), and the result lists them in words ("Edges are slightly uneven").

WHY E IS NOT AUTOMATIC
We tested 71 pairs of photos of the same unchanged mole. Two photos of one mole differed more (lighting, zoom) than a simulated 35 % darkening. Any automatic "changed" rule either cried wolf or missed real changes — so a person judges.

10The heatmap (Eigen-CAM)

The heatmap shows where the model looked in the photo. Red/yellow = where its attention was strongest; blue = little attention.

Why it is called Eigen-CAM, not Grad-CAM

Many papers use Grad-CAM, which needs "gradients" (a calculation done while training). TensorFlow Lite on the Pi cannot calculate gradients, so Grad-CAM is impossible there. EPIVUE uses Eigen-CAM (Muhammad & Yeasin, 2020), which needs only one normal forward pass. Please write "Eigen-CAM" in the paper and slides.

How it works, in four steps

  1. The photo goes through the model once.
  2. The 7 × 7 × 1280 feature map is read out (section 5).
  3. Eigen-CAM finds the single strongest pattern across those 1,280 detectors (the "first principal component") and gives each of the 49 squares a score from 0 to 1.
  4. That 7 × 7 grid is stretched over the photo and coloured (blue → red), half photo, half colour.

"On your spot" or "off your spot"

EPIVUE checks whether the brightest point of the heatmap falls inside the outline the photo check drew (with a small margin). The visitor sees:

  • "Bright area is on your spot."
  • "Bright area off your spot. For a more accurate reading, retake with the lesion highlighted in the ring." (in brown)

Staff see the percentage of all the heat that sits on the spot. It appears on the check screen after a passing photo, and as a smaller row under the ring on the result. Tap the circle to put the heatmap in the big ring, with the outline on top.

WHAT THE HEATMAP IS NOT
  • It is not an outline of the spot and not a diagnosis.
  • It shows where the model looked, not why it decided (it is "class-agnostic").
  • It is coarse: only 7 × 7 squares stretched over the photo.
  • It never changes the verdict. "Off your spot" only suggests a retake.

11Where Gemma 4 fits

Gemma 4 does not run on the Pi. The Pi has 4 GB of memory; the smallest Gemma 4 needs about 8 GB. An earlier chatbot on the Pi made scans take 30–60 seconds, so it was removed on 1 October.

Instead, Gemma 4 helps at build time on the PC: the script scripts/draft_answers_gemma.py rewords the team's approved Questions-tab answers into plainer words (keeping every timeframe). The Pi then serves those answers offline, with the badge "DRAFTED BY GEMMA 4 · NOT YET REVIEWED BY A DOCTOR" until a health professional signs them off. The Questions tab itself matches your question to a written answer (TF-IDF search); nothing is generated at the kiosk.

CURRENT STATE (30 OCT)
The drafting script has not been run yet, so the answers on the kiosk are the team's own wording. When it is run, the badge appears.
HOW TO SAY IT
"Gemma 4 drafted the plain-language answers on our development PC. The team checked them, and the device serves them offline. No text is generated on the device, so it cannot make things up in front of a patient."

12Privacy, offline and the website

  • Offline. The scan never uses the internet. No accounts, no names, no tracking.
  • Saved scans stay on the Pi's memory card (~/.dermascan/saved/) until staff press "End event", which empties them.
  • The only internet use is staff-only "Update from GitHub" in Settings, with the kiosk stopped.

13What it cannot do — say this out loud

  • It is not a diagnosis. Only a doctor (often with a biopsy) can diagnose skin cancer.
  • It will be wrong sometimes. On photos it had never seen, it missed about 1 in 5 cancers and sent about 4 in 10 harmless spots for a check.
  • It was trained mostly on dermoscopy (85 % of photos) and mostly on photos from hospitals in Europe, the USA and Australia, where most patients have lighter skin. How well it works on darker skin and on the Pi's own camera has not yet been measured.
  • It only knows three labels. It has no "not a spot" answer — that is why the photo check exists, and why non-spots give no verdict.
  • D and E are not measured automatically.
  • Some photo-check limits are guesses until the Pi photos from 30 October are measured.
  • The heatmap shows where, not why.
IF SOMEONE ASKS "SO IS IT ACCURATE?"
"On photos from hospitals it had never seen, it flagged 81 % of cancers. That is good for a screening aid, but it means it should always send people to a doctor when unsure, and never tell anyone a spot is safe. That's how we designed the wording."
